<p class="formulation-section">Assignment question</p>

> Q.If we could travel into the past, would we be able to change what has already happened? If your answer is 'yes,' explain how it would be possible to undo past happenings. And if your answer is 'no,' explain why not.

Drawing on the contentions of Lewis, Read, Vihvelin and others, as well as classical laws of thought, this essay contends that time-travel into the past with the aim of changing events in history is not possible, or at best is highly improbable. Three key logical flaws support this: identity; non-contradiction; and excluded middle.

## The Approach

The question of whether or not it would be possible to change events in the past from what has already happened, assuming time travel were possible, can be approached in two different ways.

<div class="approach-list">
  <div>
    <span>1</span>
    <p><strong>Empirical question.</strong> Whether or not it is possible in accordance to the laws of nature, which is essentially an empirical question rather than a philosophical one, and therefore best answered through experiment and scientific enquiry.</p>
  </div>

  <div>
    <span>2</span>
    <p><strong>Philosophical question.</strong> On the other hand, the question of whether or not time travel is permissible in accordance with the laws of logic and metaphysics is one that philosophy is uniquely suited to answer.</p>
  </div>
</div>


It is this latter question that I will attempt to answer.

## Linguistics of Time

The similarity in the language used to describe temporal and spatial dimensions, and the false equivalence it creates between the two, is at the heart of the first logical problem of time travel: the law of identity. According to Read (2012), this linguistic sleight of hand might be the cause for many of the problems that arise when discussing the possibility of time-travel. There is no contradiction from the statement that: I was at point (a) at one moment, went to point (b) at another, then returned to the previous point (a) again later on. It is both logically permissible and can be shown possible through experience. In contrast, when we replace the spatial coordinates within the statement with temporal coordinates we instantly create problems – a contradiction is necessarily created at point (a) – there would now be two versions of yourself at a point in time where there was only ever one. Either the past is the way it was, or it was always the way it appears to be after you have travelled back to (a); it can't be both.

## Time-Paradoxes

Such logical problems lay at the heart of all time-travel stories. The second problem of note is the law of non-contradiction. In 'The Grandfather Paradox', Lewis (1976) argues that one possible way for time-travel to occur, but without the need for paradoxes, could be if someone went back in time to kill their grandfather, but didn’t go through with it. In this context, it is true that there is some time-machine and someone has the desire to kill their grandfather, but it is false that they do kill their grandfather. Moreover, that we know this because history tells us so. While it's true that this account of the time-traveller's story doesn't involve any obvious contradiction or paradoxes, prima facie it still doesn't appear to solve the issue of reverse-causation. Contrary to Lewis, Vihvelin argues that it is impossible for someone to kill their grandfather in the past, “for ‘[someone to] kill [their] Grandfather’ to be true, there must be at least some occasions on which ‘If [they] had tried to kill [their] Grandfather, [they] would or at least might have succeeded’ is true—but...at [sic] any world remotely like ours, the latter counterfactual is always false” (Vihvelin, cited by Smith, 2013). In Lewis' account it doesn't matter how earnest someone is to kill their grandfather they can't, but it is always true they can in context of their ability and desire.

## Cause and Effect

Time travel therefore encounters the problem of reverse-causation. In our observable universe effects always appear to proceed from causes. Similarly, time also appears to have a preferred direction. Commonly referred to as the 'arrow-of-time', past events happen before future effects. Reverse-causation is where this order is reversed and causes happen after their effects. It is this reversal that is the cause of the biggest issue surrounding any form of physical time-travel into the past. As the past and present are causally connected any physical travel, even without intention to affect events, will always have a causal effect on the present and future. Indeed, even “the slightest impact upon the past will generate a ‘causal loop,’ and thus a familiar paradox of time-travel” (Read, 2012. p141).

## Causal-Loops

Lewis states that if there are scenarios where reverse causation is permitted, then it may also be possible for causal loops to exist. These causal-loop stories would appear as being both effect and a cause depending on where in the story you start. An example is presented by Law (2003) where Superman travels into the future, retrieves a book that a friend of his is yet to write then gives it to him in the present before he wrote it in the first place. His friend then copies the book and submits it for publication. When Superman brings his friend’s completed book back from the future he is creating a situation of reverse-causation. This raises the obvious question: who wrote the first book? Or more precisely: where is the entry-point for the information into the loop? It is one thing to ask where an event comes from, it is another thing to ask where an entire chain-of-events comes from. As Lewis (1976, p.149) states, while each event along the causal chain is reasonably explained by the previous one without contradiction, “the whole of it is not”. The major and inescapable problem for any causal-loop story is the need to explain how information is created ex nihilo in the first instance.

Lewis' solution is to say that while it would be strange, it might be possible for information to literally 'come from nothing.' That explaining a causal-loop’s seeming lack of informational entry point is no more or less problematic than any other kind of chain-of-events (Lewis 1976, p.149). In other words, if we go far enough back along any particular series of causes and effects we inevitably reach the same aporia: how was the first instance of the chain created? This solution isn't very satisfactory as it only deflects the problem rather than solve it. Moreover, the idea of there existing real uncaused causes is still a controversial one. Mellor (1991) argues that causal-loops are simply impossible on grounds that causality must always progress in the same direction. Horwich (1987) argues that while they may in principle be possible, due to physical constraints caused by the extreme energy requirements needed for such a trip, they are events of high improbability near enough to impossible in practical terms. This is the third logical problem: the law of excluded middle.

## In Summation

Using classical laws of thought as a guide, three logical problems arise when critically analysing backwards time-travel, and whether it is possible to change events in the past. Firstly, the ambiguity of the language used to discuss time means there is a problem with the law of identity. Secondly, because time events both do and don't happen concurrently, there are problems with the law of non-contradiction. Thirdly, the fact that information appears ex nihilo means are problems with the law of excluded middle. In addition, both of which appear to be implications caused by the breaking of causal-asymetries via reverse-causation. Using and Both Occam's razor, and the principle of 'negation as failure' therefore suggest that backward time travel is not possible. Until time-travel is either ruled out completely or proven true on physical grounds, there will continue to be fundamental problems that leave us aporetic. While time-travel is deeply counter-intuitive and raises serious problems requiring further investigation, prima facie it appears that travel into the past, let alone affecting events while there, is impossible, or at least highly improbable.
