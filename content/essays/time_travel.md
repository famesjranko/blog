The question of whether or not time travel into the past is possible can be approached in two ways.

1. Evidentiary reasoning; testing natures’ permissibility for the reversing of time.
2. Philosophical reasoning; examining its truth or falsity via logical coherency.

This brief discussion will employ the second approach, examining logical problems that arise by the proposition of time travel to the past; such as the reversal of causation, the emergence of causal loops, and the deficiencies within ordinary language to describe time.

## 1. Cause and Effect

In our observable universe effects always appear to proceed from prior causes, where every event has a preceding cause; which itself is an effect of a further preceding cause, ad infinitum. This is commonly referred to as the principle of causation and it appears to be a fundamental of nature.  Moreover, causation only ever goes in one direction, from the past to the future. A world in which causation is free to move backwards would look very different to the world we currently observe. If we did live in such a world it wouldn’t be strange to observe events such as an eggs being unscrambled, chocolate and milk being separated from a milkshake, or a window being unbroken by a rock. Modern video recording has allowed us to easily see what this reversible world would look like and it’s an oddly strange one that challenges our perception. These examples showcase the relationship that exists between time and causation; time, like causation, appears to have a preferred direction, commonly referred to as the arrow of time. And with modern recording tech, we’re able to wind the clock backwards and reverse the causal direction.

A key problem of travelling to the past is the problem of reverse-causation. Reverse-causation is where this order is reversed and an events cause happens after its effect. It this reversal that is the cause of the biggest issue surrounding any form of physical time travel; as the past and present are causally connected, any physical travel will always have a causal effect on the present and future. This is due to introducing a physical object into the past that wasn’t there when the events that lead to the current time-line happened. We tend to only think of time travel scenarios with specific incidences of large enough proportion to create specific problems, but this is fundamentally true for any interaction within a past that is causally connected to the present.

>“For the slightest impact upon the past will generate a “causal loop,” and thus a familiar paradox of time travel.” (Read 2012)

## 2. Causal Loops

If scenarios where reverse causation is permitted exist, then it may also be possible for causal loops to exist. These causal loops would exhibit events as being both effect and a cause depending on where in the loop you started the clock. An example of such a scenario is presented by Steven Law in his book, The Philosophy Gym (2003):

1. Superman has a friend who is trying to write a biography on him, but is struggling to finish the book.
2. Superman feels bad for his friend and decides to travel into the future where the book is completed and available at the local book store.  He buys a copy then brings it back to the present and gives it to his struggling writer friend.
3. His friend then copies out his own work, which he has yet to write, and submits it for publication.

When Superman brings his friends completed book back from the future he is creating a situation of reverse-causation – where an effect in the past (the writing of Superman’s biography) is now caused by an event in the future. This raises the obvious question: who wrote the first Superman biography? Or more precisely: where is the entry-point for the information into the loop?  It is one thing to ask where an event comes from; it is another thing to ask where an entire chain of events comes from… As Lewis points out, each event along the causal chain is reasonably explained by the previous one without contradiction, though, “the whole of it is not” (1976 p.149). The inescapable problem for any causal loop scenario is the need to explain how information is apparently created ex nihilo. Simply put, if the scenario has no beginning and no end, then from where was it caused?

Lewis’ solution is to say that while it would be strange, it might be possible for information to literally come from nothing. That explaining a causal loop’s apparent lack of information entry point is no more or less problematic than any other chain of events.

>“Almost everyone agrees that God, or the Big Bang, or the entire infinite past of the universe, or the decay of a tritium atom, is uncaused and inexplicable. Then if these are possible, why not also the inexplicable causal loops that arise in time travel?” (Lewis 1976)

In other words, if we go far enough back along any particular series of causes and effects we inevitably reach an aporia, where our explanatory powers ultimately run out. This solution isn’t very satisfactory as it seems to only deflect the problem rather than solve it.  In essence, it’s a non-answer to the problem. Moreover, the idea that there exist real uncaused causes is a highly controversial one. Contra Lewis, Mellor (1998) argues that causal loops are simply impossible on grounds that causality must always progress in the same direction. Additionally, Horwich (1987) argues that while they may in principle be possible, due to physical constraints caused by the extreme energy requirements needed, they are events of high improbability near enough to impossible in practical terms.

## 3. The Grandfather Paradox

There are further causal loops that are of different types.  One example is the famous Grandfather Paradox. The Grandfather Paradox is a type of causal loop, where instead of lacking an obvious starting point, such as the example of who wrote Superman’s autobiography exposed, creates a contradiction of possible outcomes dependent on events transpiring after the initial action. In this case, rather than needing to explain where the causal chain of events begins, we are faced with the paradox that what we do after effects what happens before. In short, the paradox is caused by the apparent reversal of causal events, where effects precede their causes.

A general outline of the Grandfather Paradox is as follows:
>There is a student named Dave, who after years of research invents a functioning         time-machine. Then, for whatever reason, decides to travel back in time to kill his grandfather. Using his time machine, Dave travels back to a time before his grandfather had met his grandmother and shoots him with a gun, killing him instantly. Now since his grandfather was killed before meeting his grandmother, his father is never born and therefore neither is he. Now given that Dave was never born, how could he have invented a time-machine and gone back in time to kill his own grandfather in the first place?

This is not an easy question to answer. Ultimately, it forces us to question our basic intuitions of cause and effect, and moreover, our understanding of and experience of time itself.

There have been several attempts to solve the contradiction within this paradox. For instance, Lewis argues that one possible solution to the paradox is simply that Dave never kills his grandfather. In this version of the story, Dave has a functioning time-machine and does use it to travel back in time with the intent to kill his grandfather, but for some reason he doesn’t go through with it. While this solution doesn’t explicitly close the door on the possibility of backwards time travel, by arguing that it’s true that Dave has a time-machine and the desire to kill his grandfather, but that it’s false that he kills his grandfather, it still doesn’t appear to resolve the bigger issue of reverse-causation. As Dave is still physically travelling to a time where he never was previously, his physical interactions with that time are caused by events in the future.

Essentially, there is a non-corroboration of times. While events flow causally from the past to the future from Dave’s perspective – he is born, graduates college, invents a time machine, travels back in time, then after which returns back to a time after he left – an objective historical view of time shows events at one time being caused by events yet to happen. For instance, Dave’s appearance at the time before his grandfather has sired his father happens before Dave is born, before he graduates college, and before he invents a time machine itself. It is this distinction and non-corroboration between time and time that is the hallmark and problem of time travel.

Lastly, it has been proposed that the “many worlds” approach resolves the causal problems that arise from backwards time travel. According to the many worlds theory, every time you travel back in time you are creating a new universe. This new universe would have a consistent causal chain which everybody would remember the same way, and time and time would corroborate. It would follow that in the case of Dave’s backward time travel, Dave appears in his past and from that point history continues with the presence of Dave in it; therefore when Dave hops back into his time machine and travels back the time he left, it would be a different time than when he first left. This would be a universe where Dave was never born. Once he travels back in time he is forever disconnected from his own causal chain and now becomes a part of this new universe’s causal reference.

With this approach the causal chain is upheld, we avoid paradoxes and don’t need to explain where the causal of apparent causal loops. In essence, Dave is not changing his past, but creating a new past and forever removing himself from his original reference point.  Therefore avoiding any issues of reverse causation, as the time Dave leaves is not the future of the time Dave effects.

Though, while this approach does resolve the causal problems association with backwards time travel, it’s hard to fully accept this time of travel as time travel in the classic sense. While this may evidentially be the case if backwards time travel was ever to be tested, it is essentially beyond the scope of a philosophical approach to the problem. At the point of testing such a scenario we have crossed from philosophical reasoning to evidentiary reasoning; from philosophy to science.

## 4. The Linguistics of Time

Language has the capacity to trick us into attaching intuitive understandings to counter intuitive concepts. For instance, the terms used for events in time are the same as those used for events in space. We refer to time as moving, passing, and standing still. This shared vocabulary between time and space fools us into presupposing that temporal events share similar qualities to spatial events. According to Read (2012) it is this linguistic sleight of hand that might be the cause for many, if not all of the problems which arise when discussing the possibility of time travel.

Take for example the statement:
>I was at point (a) at specific moment, went to point (b) at another, and then returned at a later time (a1).

This statement exhibits no contradiction as long as the times of each event are different. This is both logically permissible and demonstrably possible through experience. It is only when the times of event (a) and (a1) match that a contradiction is created, as there are now two existing moments at the same time and place that disagree on events. In essence there are two distinct and separate historical realities coexisting at (a) at the same time:

1. The history when you were on your own before embarking on your journey
2. The history where there are two versions of you present.

Since we can’t simply ‘walk’ back in time to see if events have changed, we must assume history to be as it is revealed to us through experience and documentation. This leads us to two permissible possibilities:

1. Time travel is not permitted and the past is forever inaccessible
2. Time travel is permitted and therefore history should exhibit contradictions.

As the notion of time travel is counter intuitive and devoid from experience, and we have to assume contradictions can’t exist, we are therefore forced to accept that time travel is both logically and experientially impermissible.

It is here that I will end this discussion.
